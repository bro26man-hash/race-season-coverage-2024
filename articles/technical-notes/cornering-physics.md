# Cornering Physics: G-Force Reference Notes

> Sourced from Wikipedia's **[G-force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-force)** article, extracted for race season cornering analysis.

## What Is G-Force?

- **G-force (gravitational force equivalent)** is a mass-specific force (force per unit mass), expressed in units of standard gravity (symbol **g** or **g₀**, not to be confused with "g" for grams).
- **1 g** = standard gravitational acceleration at Earth's surface ≈ **9.80665 m/s²** (≈ 35.3 km/h per second).
- G-force is technically a measure of **acceleration**, not force. It is produced only by **mechanical contact forces** — gravity alone does not produce g-force.
- Whenever a vehicle changes **direction** or **speed**, occupants feel **lateral (side-to-side)** or **longitudinal (forward-backward)** forces produced by the mechanical push of their seats.

## Lateral G-Loads Through Corners

- When a vehicle changes direction (corners), the occupants experience **lateral g-forces** — side-to-side forces produced by the mechanical push of the seat.
- Lateral g-force is the **horizontal acceleration** perpendicular to the direction of travel, experienced through cornering.
- The expression "1 g = 9.80665 m/s²" means that for every second, velocity changes by ~35 km/h. A car braking at 1 g from 35 km/h can stop in **one second**; from 105 km/h, in **three seconds**.
- For a given speed **v** and corner radius **r**, the centripetal acceleration is **v²/r**, which directly determines the lateral g-load experienced.

## Centripetal Acceleration

- **Centripetal acceleration** is the acceleration directed toward the center of a curved path, responsible for changing the direction of velocity during cornering.
- The magnitude of centripetal acceleration is **a = v²/r**, where **v** is speed and **r** is the radius of curvature.
- This acceleration must be produced by a **mechanical force** (tire grip, downforce) — it cannot be produced by gravity alone.
- In a corner, the g-force felt by the driver and car is the **vector sum** of all gravitational and non-gravitational forces acting on the car's freedom to move.
- The **faster the speed** or **tighter the radius**, the greater the centripetal acceleration and the higher the lateral g-load.

## The Role of Downforce

- **Downforce** is the aerodynamic force that pushes the car downward into the track, increasing the **normal force** between tires and road.
- Increased normal force → increased **tire grip** (friction) → ability to sustain higher **lateral g-loads** through corners without sliding.
- Downforce allows race cars to generate cornering forces far exceeding what tire friction alone could provide at lower speeds.
- At high speeds, aerodynamic downforce can be several times the car's own weight, effectively increasing the "gravity" the car experiences through corners.
- The g-force experienced in a corner is directly proportional to the lateral force; downforce multiplies the available lateral force, enabling higher cornering speeds.

## Physical Demands on Drivers

- **Human tolerance** to g-forces depends on: **magnitude**, **duration**, **direction**, **location of application**, and **posture**.
- The human body is **better at surviving g-forces perpendicular to the spine** (horizontal/eyeballs-in) than along the spine (vertical).
- Untrained humans can tolerate approximately:
  - **20 g₀** for less than 10 seconds
  - **10 g₀** for 1 minute
  - **6 g₀** for 10 minutes
- **Positive vertical g-force** (force blood toward feet) progression causes: grey-out → tunnel vision → blackout → G-LOC (g-force induced loss of consciousness) → death if not reduced.
- Typical person can handle about **5 g₀** before losing consciousness; modern pilots with g-suits and straining can sustain **9 g₀**.
- **Negative g-force** (blood to head) tolerance is much lower: typically **−2 to −3 g₀**.
- **"Eyeballs-out" horizontal g-force** (acceleration backward) is more dangerous than "eyeballs in" due to retinal blood vessel sensitivity.
- **Record peak horizontal g-force survived**: **214 g₀** — Kenny Bräck, 2003 IndyCar Series finale at Texas Motor Speedway (crash impact).
- **John Stapp's record**: survived **46.2 g₀** peak "eyeballs-out" acceleration and **25 g₀** for 1.1 seconds in rocket sled experiments.
- G-tolerance can be **partially trained**; there is considerable **individual variation**; cardiovascular problems **reduce** g-tolerance.
- In racing, sustained lateral g-loads of **4–6 g** are typical through high-speed corners; drivers must maintain **physical conditioning** and **anti-G straining maneuvers** to stay conscious and perform.

## Key Formulae

| Concept | Formula | Description |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| Standard gravity | g₀ = 9.80665 m/s² | Acceleration at Earth's surface |
| Centripetal acceleration | a = v²/r | Lateral g-load determined by speed and radius |
| Braking distance | s = v²/(2a) | Distance to stop at a given deceleration |
| Weight under g-force | W = m × g-force | Apparent weight under acceleration |

## Race Context

- **Dragster**: can exert horizontal g-force of **5.3 g** during acceleration.
- **Typical racing cornering**: sustained lateral g-loads of 4–6 g in high-speed corners.
- **Crash impacts**: can produce **instantaneous g-forces** far exceeding sustained values (e.g., 214 g₀ in Bräck's crash).
